Mobility, Enterprise SaaS

Telematics Platform Transformation

Telematics SaaS platform, rebuilt for a Japanese automotive enterprise client, delivered through an IT consulting engagement.

Chapter 1 — The Fragments

A few initial meetings in, before I'd proposed anything, I sat, watched, and listened. Ideas clashed, with people defending decisions while others tore them apart, and underneath the noise I was trying to work out what had actually gone wrong. Functions existed without much sense of why a user would actually need them, built to technical spec rather than around any real task or intent. Content had no hierarchy — data sat on the page with no order to signal what mattered or how one piece connected to another. None of it was held together by a design system or any real quality control. The legacy codebase compounded it; performance and stability had degraded to the point that a rebuild from zero was the only realistic path.

All of that was a craft problem — the kind structure and better design could solve. The real problem was the one I didn't see coming. Meeting after meeting meant introducing UX concepts the client had never had reason to consider, clearing up a misunderstanding, or pushing back on an idea that sounded reasonable but ran against basic UX principles. Getting the design right was going to be half the job. The other half was this.

Chapter 2 — The Hundred Frames

The clearest test came when I proposed merging several data-heavy pages into one. They were related and interconnected, and splitting them across separate screens forced users to reconstruct context that should have lived in one place. On paper, it was the right call. In the room, it wasn't obvious at all.

Stakeholders worried the rework would stretch the sprint and the budget with it. The development team resisted for a different reason: the existing pages worked, and rebuilding something functional felt like unnecessary risk. This was my first project on the account, and if I couldn't bring both sides around, it would raise real questions about whether I was ready for a client of this size.

Instead of arguing the concept upfront, I built it in front of them, in stages, over the following months. Several heavy features had to be reconciled into one page, and each round of progress went back to stakeholders and the development team before the next began. Feedback came in every session, some of it hard to hear, and I folded it in before returning with the next stage. I took on the core of the page myself early, since I was the one who'd answer for it if the room turned against it.

Once the direction held and reactions became more predictable, I brought in other designers for the remaining sub-features. What looked like a single page in the product was close to a hundred Figma frames once every condition, state, and action was accounted for, and no single person was finishing that alone in a reasonable timeframe. My job shifted to making sure their work met the same objective and never dropped the experience quality the concept depended on, since the client saw one name behind the account regardless of who drew which screen. I stayed the one presenting and defending every decision in the room.

By the time the full page was ready, the conversation had shifted from whether this was a good idea to what it would take to finish it. Some data wasn't clean enough to merge yet, so we drew a line: what could ship, shipped, and what couldn't went into a backlog for later. The page that shipped was smaller than what I'd first proposed, but the seamlessness that mattered held.

Chapter 3 — The Long Game

The client didn't distinguish between who was responsible for quality — a designer, a QA team, a third-party vendor. They only cared that what shipped matched what had been proposed in Figma. So when the third-party QA wasn't catching issues at the standard we needed, closing that gap wasn't optional, it was mine to close. I set up a review layer of our own. My team checked the staging site before anything went to production, screenshotted bugs or inaccurate output, described the expected behavior against it, and tagged me directly. If the development team hit something they couldn't solve, or got blocked, I wanted to know immediately rather than after it shipped.

Two years on, the backlog that came out of that first compromise is steady, not because the work slowed down, but because we learned our real capacity and stopped absorbing everything that came our way. That mattered more than it might sound, because the rebuild itself was still expanding — new user roles kept entering scope, and new feature requests kept arriving on top of that. What started as a one-year engagement is now in its fourth year. That wasn't the plan. New features and new user roles kept coming, one after another, each one leading to another contract renewal. Nobody set out to build a four-year relationship. It's what happened when a client kept finding more reasons to keep building with us.
